Breakdown of phosphatidylinositol was studied in homogenates and subcellular fractions of rat cerebral cortex by using both membrane-bound and externally added [32P]phosphatidylinositol as substrate. 2. In the presence of deoxycholate breakdown followed first-order kinetics at low substrate concentrations (<1mM) and zero-order kinetics at higher concentrations (6-9mM). 3. Maximum breakdown by cerebral-cortex homogenates was approximately 0.5,umol/h per mg ofprotein and occurred at pH7.0 in the presence of 8mM-phosphatidylinositol, 2mM-CaCl2 and 2mg of deoxycholate/ml. Activity was abolished by 1mM-ethanedioxybis(ethylamine)tetra-acetate. 4. The products of phosphatidylinositol breakdown were 1,2-diacylglycerol and a mixture of D-myoinositol 1:2-cyclic phosphate (55%) and D-myoinositol 1-phosphate (45%). The two phosphate esters appeared to be produced together and in constant proportions. 5. Some 51 % ofthe activity was particle-bound, with the highest activities in small nerve endings, microsomal material and two synaptic membrane fractions (fractions Mic20, Mic100, Ml 1.0 and Ml 0.9 respectively), all of which were also rich in acetylcholinesterase and which have been shown to be rich in other surface-membrane enzymes. Much of the particle-bound activity therefore appears to be present in cerebral-cortex plasma membranes. 6. The results are discussed in relation to previously described soluble activities that catalyse the same reaction, and to a possible role of the membrane-bound enzyme in enhanced phosphatidylinositol turnover in externally stimulated cells.

There have been several reports of enzymes from animal tissues that degrade phosphatidylinositol. These have usually been soluble cytoplasmic activities that initially appeared to act as phosphatidylinositol inositolphosphohydrolases (reaction 1); they require Ca2+ and have acid pH optima (Kemp et al., 1961; Atherton & Hawthorne, 1968; Friedel et al., 1967, 1969; Thompson, 1967). Recent work by Dawson and his colleagues (Jungalwala et al., 1971; Dawson et al., 1971) has shown that these enzymes are cyclizing phosphotransferases that also catalyse reaction (2), the release from phosphatidylinositol of D-myoinositol 1:2-cyclic phosphate.

We have now detected a membrane-bound enzyme activity in rat cerebral cortex that releases myoinositol 1:2-cyclic phosphate from phosphatidylinositol. Some characteristics of this enzyme and its subcellular distribution are reported in this paper. A brief description of this work has been published (Michell & Lapetina, 1972).

The reaction catalysed by the membrane-bound enzyme reported here is clearly similar to that catalysed by the previously described soluble enzymes, being a Ca2+-dependent cleavage of phosphatidylinositol between the phosphate group and the glycerol backbone, with release of 1,2-diacylglycerol and a mixture ofD-myoinositol 1:2-cyclic phosphate and D-myoinositol 1-phosphate. the soluble enzymes which are most active at pH values between 5 and 6. For maximum activity to be expressed against either membrane-bound lipid or externally added lipid the particulate enzyme requires the presence of an anionic detergent, deoxycholate. In the presence of the detergent, substrate from both sources is treated similarly. It is not clear whether the main effect ofthe detergent is to disrupt the membranes, allowing access of substrate to the enzyme, or to improve the dispersion or the surface charge properties of the substrate. In contrast the soluble enzyme, at least in intestinal mucosa, is strongly inhibited by detergents (Atherton & Hawthorne, 1968). The phosphatidylinositol concentrations needed for maximum activity ofthe membranebound activity (6-9mM) is much higher than the requirement of the soluble enzymes (1-3mM; Kemp et al., 1961; Thompson, 1967; Atherton & Hawthorne, 1968). However, it is not clear what a substrate optimum for a membrane enzyme under such conditions might mean in physiological terms, as we lack evidence to indicate the state in which the substrate is presented to the enzyme inside the cell.

Under the optimum zero-order assay conditions reported here, 51% of the activity was membranebound. The 49% that was soluble was not studied further, and we have not determined whether this is the previously described cerebral-cortex soluble activity (Thompson, 1967; Friedel et al., 1967, 1969) or a new activity that is active at pH7.0 and which is not inhibited by detergents. The membrane-bound activity was concentrated in fractions (Mic20, Micloo, M1 0.9, Ml 1.0) known from previous work on the subcellular distribution of 5'-nucleotidase, acetylcholinesterase, adenyl cyclase and Na+ + K+stimulated adenosine triphosphatase (Lapetina & Hawthorne, 1971; De Robertis, 1967), and from their content of acetylcholinesterase in our experiments, to be enriched in plasma-membrane fragments (Fig. 4). The data, as with any studies of subcellular fractions from brain, are not sufficiently clear-cut to assign activity solely to any one intracellular site, but they do indicate that much of the membrane-bound activity carrying phosphatidylinositol breakdown in cerebral cortex isprobably incellsurface-membranes. Lapetina & Hawthorne, 1971) were almost certainly due to a rather large number of unbroken cells, leading to a high amount of entrapped soluble material.

The particulate activity described here is clearly similar, but produces slightly less (about 55%) D-myoinositol 1:2-cyclic phosphate at its optimum pH of7.0. Possible role of the membrane-bound enzyme in stimulatedphosphatidylinositol turnover

Thepossibleroleofenzymescleavingphosphatidylinositol in the reactions that lead to enhanced phosphatidylinositol labelling in a variety of stimulated tissues, including acetylcholine-treated cerebral cortex, has been discussed several times (Hokin, 1969; Durell et al., 1969; Lapetina & Hawthorne, 1971; Jungalwala et al., 1971; De Robertis, 1971; Dawson et al., 1971; Lapetina & Michell, 1972; Michell & Lapetina, 1972).

This is the first time that production of D-myoinositol 1:2-cyclic phosphate has been demonstrated as a result of a plasma membrane-bound enzyme. This observation and the recent report of a specific phosphodiesterase converting the cyclic ester (Dawson & Clarke, 1972) into inositol 1-phosphate increase the obvious parallels between the reactions involved in production and breakdown of D-myoinositol 1:2-cyclic phosphate and adenosine 3':5'cyclic phosphate, particularly as situations in which D-myoinositol 1:2-cyclic phosphate production may well increase are situations involving external stimulationofcells (Dawsonetal., 1971 ; Dawson &Clarke, 1972; Michell & Lapetina, 1972).

It has, however, always been difficult to develop a model in which phosphatidylinositol breakdown catalysed by the known soluble enzyme might participate. Durell and his collaborators have presented some evidence for the participation of a membranebound phosphatidylinositol phosphodiesterase, but have never fully characterized such an enzyme (Durell et al., 1969; Durell & Garland, 1969). It is now clear that a highly active enzyme of this type exists in cell surface-membranes in cerebral cortex. This enzyme may be the stimulated step in the cycle ofreactions leading toenhanced phosphatidylinositol labelling.
