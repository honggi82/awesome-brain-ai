THE following case shows extremely rare congenital abnormalities of the eye, and an apparently unique malformation of the brain.

Alice B. was brought to one of us (J. H. P.) during the first week of life. The parents are both healthy; the mother is 21 years old, and this is the first child; there have been no miscarriages. There is no history of malformations, &c, on either side.

The child was healthy and well nourished, born naturally at term. At birth a large dark swelling was noticed projecting from the right orbit. The child was kept under continual observation as an out-patient at the Children's Hospital, Great Ormond Street, for seven months, when it was operated upon. The condition did not vary materially, though there was some increase in the tumour. From the mother's description it would seem to have been especially prominent and dark soon after birth.

The right eye was considerably smaller than the left, though it otherwise showed no obvious external anomaly ; the cornea measured 95 mm. in diameter. The case was shown at the Ophthalmological Society when the child was 13 weeks old. A variety of suggestions were made as to the nature of the tumour, e.g., meningocele or meningo-encephalocele, hsemangioma, lymphangioma, plexiform neuroma, congenital cyst of the orbit associated with microphthalmia. Against meningocele was the fact that at this time there was no definite pulsation. The colour was against hsemangioma, and lymphangioma is so rare in the orbit that such a diagnosis was improbable. The general character of the mass and the absence of swelling over the temporal region made plexiform neuroma unlikely. The absence of a coloboma of the choroid and the relatively large size of the eye made congenital cyst associated with microphthalmia also improbable.

After consultation with my colleague, Mr. Kellock, I (J. H. P.) determined to exenterate the orbit, hoping that I might be able to close the aperture in the skull by stitching the meninges over it. The whole of the contents of the orbit were removed, including the eye and a mass of brain. The meninges were adherent to the globe and were so much attenuated that it was found impossible to close the aperture in the skull. During the following days there was a large amount of leakage of cerebro-spinal fluid, the dressings and pillow being soaked. The child died on the fourth day.

At the post-mortem examination the cranial cavity was found to communicate with the right orbit by a large hole, 10 mm. in diameter, below the lesser wing of the sphenoid ; it obviously represented the combined optic foramen and inner part of the sphenoidal fissure.

There are two theories which might account for the development of a large isolated mass of brain substance such as that described. It is possible that it might be the teratological development of a second brain. It is more probable, in our opinion, that at an early period in foetal life a small portion of potential cortical brain substance was separated more or less from the main mass and grew and developed independently, finally becoming completely isolated. Professor Cunningham suggests from the absence of any central cavity and the position of the stalk of pia mater—near the anterior perforated space—that there was originally some connection with the caudate nucleus region.

This interesting globe shows no fewer than three different congenital abnormalities, each of which is most unusual.

- (1) With regard to the staphylomatous bulging on the nasal side of the nerve, its position is its most notable feature. " Colobomata of the choroid " in any position except downwards and in the macular region are exceedingly uncommon, and not a few of the published cases suggest patches of old choroido-retinitis rather than true congenital abnormalities. They have, however, been seen outwards (Adams Frost [1] —a case with a coloboma of the iris also outwards), and upwards and outwards (Rindfleisch [26], Nael and Leplat [21], Nuel? [20], Fuchs [8]). It is worthy of note that atypical coloboma of the iris may be associated with coloboma of the choroid in the usual position (v. Mittelstadt [22], v. Eeuss [25], Hess [1-4]). We have only been able to find two cases of inward coloboma. In one of these, recorded by Randall and de Schweinitz [24], there was a deeply excavated, bluish-white defect one and a half times the diameter of the papilla, and situated inwards and a little downwards from it. In the other recorded by Steinheim [28], there was a large inward coloboma, engulfing the papilla, with a coloboma of the iris in the same position. We believe the present case to be the first atypical coloboma (apart from macular colobomata) submitted to pathological examination. It was not found to differ in its essential microscopical details from colobomata elsewhere. As has frequently been found in other cases, especially in eyes obtained shortly after birth, there was no actual defect in any layer, but only a considerable stretching and thinning. In particular the retina was perfectly normal except for a degree of atrophy of the nerve fibre layer, which was probably to be attributed to the condition of the nerve entrance.
- (2) Several cases of coloboma of the optic nerve entrance have been examined microscopically. They fall usually into one of three classes. The most common are colobomata of the choroid reaching up to the nerve, and are therefore not true defects of the nerve itself. When these become ectatic the lower border of the nerve becomes displaced upwards and backwards, so that the surface of the papilla

The ectasia has therefore taken place to the inner side of the intervaginal space, and the latter has not been displaced with the nerve, but has retained its normal position. (3) The case furnishes a good example of a central or macular coloboma. As in the coloboma on the nasal side of the disc so here, there was no actual gap in any layer though there was defective formation. In the retina there was even an imperfectly-formed but quite recognisable fovea. This has not, we believe, been previously found in a macular coloboma. The most defective layer was the pigment epithelium. It ,was present only in islands and had entirely lost its pigment. It should be noted that neither here nor in the other colobomata was there the least evidence of old or recent inflammation. Indeed, the absence of all adhesion between the retina and the choroid is direct proof to the contrary. Hence the case adds to the already very large amount of evidence against Deutschmann's theory that all colobomata are due to inflammation in early foetal life. There was also no bulging of the ocular tunics. It seems evident, therefore, that the adhesion between retina and choroid, the extreme atrophy and distortion of these layers, and the deep excavation which have usually been found in pathological examinations of macular and other colobomata, are secondary phenomena, due to subsequent inflammation, or to stretching and giving way of a weak part of the globe. It is highly probable that they were not present in this instance because the eye was obtained from an infant, and therefore before secondary changes had occurred. Confirmation of this view is obtained from a case reported by Pause, in which the retina and choroid were both quite intact over a coloboma of the choroid, while the pigment epithelium was present, but deprived of its pigment. The case, therefore, closely resembles ours, and it occurred in a child which died at birth. Depigmentation of the pigment epithelium has also been noted by Bock and others over typical colobomata of the choroid. The changes more commonly found in macular colobomata are described in the papers of Hess [13], Bock [3], Deyl [4], and v. Duyse [5].

present in the eye were not independent of the orbital encephalocele. Had the colobomata been of the ordinary type it might have been possible to suppose that they were one expression of a general tendency towards the formation of congenital abnormalities, while the brain tumour was another expression of the same tendency, just as, for instance, supernumerary digits on the hands and feet are frequently associated, yet not dependent the one on the other. In view of the fact, however, that the brain masses in the orbit directly pressed upon and flattened the globe, and since two at least of the colobomata were of quite an atypical variety, it seems much more likely that they stood in the relation of cause and effect. Moreover, micropbthalmia with colobomata has been found associated with contraction of the orbit from hydrocephalus (Rindfleisch, Hans Virchow).

A possible explanation would be that the apparently isolated mass of brain substance had been snared off at an early period of foetal life, and had interfered with the proper budding out of the primary optic vesicle, or had even been derived from a portion of brain substance which should have gone to the formation of the vesicle. It seems highly improbable, however, that if this had been so the globe would have been so well formed, especially in its anterior parts. Since there is no defect below either in the nerve or retina, it is evident that a secondary optic vesicle also formed and closed in the usual manner. We believe it to be certain therefore that the defects in the globe were caused by the pressure of the growing brain masses on the growing secondary optic vesicle.

The explanation of colobomata which do not occur in the line of the foetal cleft is always a matter of difficulty, but in the present instance this is not so great as usual. It was evident that the mass entering the orbit from the inner side had chiefly pressed upon the nasal side of the globe. This was shown by the marked flattening of the sclera, and intimate adhesion of the dura to it on that side, as well as by the marked displacement of the eye to the temporal side. The encephalocele had a lobulated surface, so that its
