In addition to the question as to why the great nerve tracts are crossed, everyone who has looked at the plan of the cerebral connexions with the various parts of the body must have asked himself why the various motor and sensory areas are arranged in so remarkable a manner on the cerebral cortex. Why is the area for the foot and leg near the upper border of the cerebrum, that for the arm and hand lower down, and that for the neck and head lower still ?

I believe that the important and most interesting observations recently published by Colonel Lister, C.M.G., and Lieut.-Colonel Gordon Holmes, C.M.G., ~ and later still by Lieut.-Colonel Gordon Holmes, afford a clue to the answer to this question. These authors, from their observations on a long series of cases in which disturbances of the field of vision had resulted from injuries to the visual cortex, have established the fact that " the upper half of each retina is represented in the dorsal, and the lower half of each retina in the ventral, part of the visual cortex." This means that the area associated with the lower part of the field of vision is represented in a higher part (i.e., nearer the upper cerebral margin)of the visual

cortex than that associated with the upper part of the field of vision.
