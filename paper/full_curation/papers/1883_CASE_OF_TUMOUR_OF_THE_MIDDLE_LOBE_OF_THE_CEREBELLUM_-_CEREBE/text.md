"John E., aged 5, was admitted April 25 [1871], and was afterwards transferred to my care by Dr. [later, Sir] Andrew Clark. Copious and very careful notes of the case were taken, chiefly by Mr. [now Sir] Stephen Mackenzie; and of these the following is an abstract.

" History.—The boy had cut his teeth early. He walked at the age of thirteen months, and, except for two convulsive seizures, both on one day, when (his mother said) he was cutting a top tooth, he had been healthy. Four months before admission, the illness of which he died began. The first things noticed were, that the boy's head became so much larger that his father's cap would fit him, and that he complained of pain at the back of his head. He cried a good deal, and lost flesh. Two months later, he began to be ' stupid.' Fourteen days before admission, his legs began to fail in walking [see later a note on reeling, under the heading ' State of Limbs '] ; aud, two or three days before admission, he began to pass urine and faeces without notice."

When admitted, he seemed to be in good general health. He took his food well ; and, although his mother said he had become thinner, he was well nourished. [1906. I believe that the reel in an early stage of disease of the cerebellum is owing to weakness of the muscles (properly loss of some of their movements), which brace up the spine, a suggestion made long ago by Niemeyer. It is true that the legs in an early stage of cerebellar disease, when there is reeling, act erratically, but they are not in fault. To speak metaphorically, they are not only doing their duty, but are doing more than what is in health required of them ; the abnormalities of their motions are praiseworthy attempts to prop up the trunk and, so to say, " to run after it" in the various over-inclinings of it which are consequent on loss of some of the movements of the spinal muscles. The motions of the legs in a reeling man are better called " snper-normal " than "abnormal."]

The attitude depicted in that sketch (and so for the second sketch) is a caricature, with gross exaggeration in some particulars, of the attitude depicted in a snapshot of a man or a child running very fast. "REMARKS.—[1871]. The diagnosis in this case was not difficult. There were three chief symptoms: (1) enlargement of the head ; (2) blindness ; and (3) reeling gait. Each of these symptoms, by itself, would be of uncertain value in diagnosis. The enlargement of the head might be owing to an [to a voluminous] adventitious product in the cerebrum—a large hydatid cyst, for instance. In such a case [in a young child] there might be an irregular gait, very like the reeling which was observed in the boy's case ; but there would be, if not decided heniiplegia, much more weakness of one side of the body than of the other. The blindness from optic neuritis was of no value as to the position of the lesion, because tumour, in very many parts of the encephalon, gives rise to double optio neuritis [and thereby to defect or loss of sight]. Perhaps, of the three symptoms, the reeling gait was of most value, but by itself would only point to tumour under the tentoriuni [would only localise to that extent].
