An important conclusion of the present study is that 8 receptors occur at high density only in the forebrain and in two midbrain structures, whereas ,u and K receptors exhibit discrete patterns in most major brain regions.

###### METHODS

Autoradiography. Autoradiographic studies were carried out using male Sprague-Dawley rats (150-200g) as describedby us (12, 13). ForA-receptorlabeling, brain sectionswereincubated at 40C for 1 hr in 50 mM TrisHCl (pH 7.4) containing 4 nM [3H][D-Ala2,N-MePhe4,Gly-o15]Enk to assess total binding. 8 receptors were labeled with 6 nM [3H][D-Pen2,D-Pen5]Enk in the same buffer (40C for 3 hr). For K-receptor labeling, brain sectionswereincubatedinbuffer(40Cfor1 hr)containing10nM [3H]EKC in [D-Ala2,N-MePhe4,Gly-o15]Enk (100 nM) and [DAla2,D-Leu5]Enk(100nM)toblockradioligandbindingtoAuand 8receptors and to restrict its binding to K receptors. Increasing the blocker concentrations to 1 AM does not change the Kd or B,[. Thisfindingindicatesthatresidualbindingof[3H]EKCto A and 8 receptors is <10%6. The absence of At and 8 opioid receptors in many brain areas, shown here by our quantitative autoradiographicanalyses, ensuresthe specificityoflabelingby [3H]EKC. For all ligands, adjacent sections were incubated under the same conditions in a solution containing radioligand in a 1000-fold excess ofthe corresponding nonlabeled ligand to assess nonspecific binding. These radioligand binding conditions have been shown to label opioid receptors quantitatively and with the expected pharmacological characteristics (11). Nonspecific binding in every case was <10 + 2% of total binding.

The actions of opiates and opioid peptides upon nervous tissue are mediated through ,u, 8, and K opioid receptors and therelated acreceptor(forreviews, seerefs. 1 and2). Thefour receptor classes exhibit different ligand selectivity patterns and mediate distinct physiological and behavioral effects. An importantquestionis whetherdifferent opioidreceptors have distinct distributions in the central nervous system. Early studies used [3H]naloxone (3, 4), [3H]dihydromorphine (5), or radiolabeled enkephalin (Enk) analogs (3, 5, 6) andqualitative autoradiography to depict the distribution of ,u and 8 opioid receptors in rat brain at the level of the light microscope. Theirfindings suggested that ,u and 8receptors occur in most brain regions. These studies were, however, limited by the unavailability ofspecific radioligands capable ofdiscriminatingbetween the tworeceptors. Goodman and Snyder (7) used [3H]ethylketocyclazocine (EKC) and [3H]bremazocine (potent K opioids) in the presence of selective ,u and 8 blockers to elucidate the K-receptor distribution in guinea pig brain.

Adjacent brain sections were stained with cresyl violet for histological verification of structures, and anatomical structures were identified by reference to the rat atlas ofPaxinos and Watson (14). The averaged optical density values from a minimum of three consecutive sections per rat after correction for the contributions due to nonspecific binding and to background film density were converted to receptor density values by reference to tritium standards (Amersham). Receptor density values, in fmol/mg of tissue, are reported as means ± SEM of averaged values from the corresponding frozen sections of a minimum of three rats.

The present study differs from previous autoradiographic studies of opioid receptorlocalization inthatradioligands highly specific foreach opioid receptorwere used in aquantitative study. An important conclusion ofthe present study is that 8 receptors occurat high density only in the forebrain and in two midbrain structures, while ,u and K receptors exhibit discrete patterns in most major brain regions. Do the neuroanatomical localizations of the tt, 8, and K opioid receptors coincide with the localizations ofthe opioid peptides with which they interact? At the level of the diencephalon, Enk immunoreactivity is intense in the ventrobasal hypothalamus and amygdala and is absent from the thalamus and cerebral cortex (16). The tL and 8 opioid receptor density is high in the cortex and undetectable in the hypothalamus. Thus, in the rat hypothalamus high concentrations of opioid peptides and well-documented opioidmediated functions must be reconciled with the virtual absence of,t and 8 receptors. This discrepancy is an example ofwhat has been termed a "mismatch" between receptorand transmitter localization (17, 18). The thalamus shows the opposite kind ofmismatch, very low levels ofopioid peptides (16) and dense u and K opioid receptors. What might account for this seeming dichotomy? Opioid peptides, as has been suggested for other neuropeptides, may act on receptors at considerable distances from their release sites.
