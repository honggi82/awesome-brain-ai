Blood Sutpply of Scalp and Skull Independent.

It is a primary condition in the formation of an osteoplastic flap that a sufficienit blood supply for the bone should be miiaintained, and with this object it has been customary to turn down the scalp with the bone in onie piece. I believe that this is quite unnecessary, and that osteoplastic flaps can be formed with equal secuirity and with far greater facility if the scalp is turned down first. The skull receives the great bulk of its blood supply through the muscles which are attached to it and from the mucous membrane which lines its cavities. The dura forms a periosteal lining, anid as such carries its own 'blood supply, but only minute vessels from this source actually enter the bone. The scalp, on the other hand, is separated from the periosteum of the calvaria by a layer of loose areolar tissue, in which there can be found only a few vessels of trivial importance, which can have no appreciable effect on the vitality of the skull itself. These points will become clearer if we consider certaini details in the anatomy of the scalp and the calvaria.

The method of applyilng the massage is a matter of the first importance for successful treatment. It slhould be directed to the panniculus adiposus, and either powder or a minimum only of lubricant should be used. With the bare hand the grip is too firm and l)ainful, and witlh much oil too slippery. The manipulation is a little difficult to describe, but very easy to demonstrate. A mass of the panniculus adiposus should be grasped witlh one or both lands, holdinig it between the thumiib and tlhenar eminence on the one side and the rest of the hand on the other side, and then the two parts of the hand approximated with adequate pressure and a slow movement. Tlle skin is usually bound down to the subjacent tissues, and it too should be grasped and manipulated with the object of loosening it and making it more flexible. Many patients can be taught to treat themselves to a certain extent.
