## BRAIN-STIMULATION INTENSITY., RATE OF SELFSTIMULATION, AND REINFORCEMENT STRENGTH: AN ANALYSIS THROUGH CHAINING'

T. DARYL HAWKINS AND STANLEY S. PLISKOFF

UNIVERSITY OF MARYLAND

Reinforcement strengths of different intensities of brain stimulation were assessed by means of a two-member behavioral chain. A variable interval schedule of 30 sec was the first-member, and five lever presses, each rewarded with stimulation, was the second. It was found that response rate on the VI schedule continued to increase beyond the intensity value which produced peak rate on the second-member, self-stimulation lever. It was concluded (1) that brain-stimulation reinforcement strength cannot be assessed adequately by means of selfstimulation rate, and (2) the chaining technique employed in the present experiment appears promising as an analytical tool in brain-stimulation research. Finally, some aspects of the data suggested a fatigue or stimulation-adaptation phenomenon.

It is apparent that other techniques for assessing reinforcement strength are necessary to attack the problem. Hodos and Valenstein (1962) employed a preference test and concluded that self-stimulation rate does not adequately reflect reinforcement strength. Another technique, the one employed here, involves the use of a two-member behavioral chain (Ferster and Skinner, 1957). A twomember behavioral chain, with self-stimulation in the second member, permits a comparison between self-stimulation rate and the characteristics of first-member performance. The first member of the behavioral chain employed in the present paper was a variable interval of 30 sec, and changes in VI response rate were compared with changes in self-stimulation rate as the intensity of brain stimulation was varied&

It has been assumed that changes in the intensity of rewarding electrical stimulation of the brain alters behavioral reinforcement strength (Sidman, Brady, Boren, Conrad, and Schulman 1955). One of the earliest tests was to observe changes in self-stimulation rate on a continuously available response lever as a function of stimulation intensity. (Throughout this report, "self-stimulation rate" refers to the procedure of determining response rate when each lever press is followed by electrical stimulation with the animal pacing its own responses.) In extensive studies of the problem (e.g., Reynolds, 1958), it has been found that self-stimulation rate increases with increases in intensity up to a point. Beyond a point for each animal, self-stimulation rate declines with further increases in intensity. The question remains as to whether the decline in self-stimulation rate at higher intensities reflects a decrease in reinforcement strength and/or the development of aversive properties. Possibly the decrease in self-stimulation rate at high intensities reflects neither of those events, but rather a slowing of the rate due to some other effect of stimulation, such as seizures or respondent interference with ongoing operants. Valenstein (1964) has discussed the problem in some detail.

METHOD

The final baseline chain performance was as follows. The left-hand lever was continuously available while the right-hand lever was retracted out of the box. The reinforcing strength of a stimulus can be defined in several ways. Chaining procedures, as suggested by Kelleher and Gollub (1962), offer a relatively sensitive, meaningful and versatile method of assessing reinforcement strength, inasmuch as changes in the reinforcement strength of a given member of a chain can produce easily detectable changes in behavior during earlier members. The use of the chaining procedure described above indicates clearly that rewarding electrical stimulation of the brain increases in reinforcement strength in the range of intensities which produce lowered self-stimulation rates. The lowering of the self-stimulation rate at higher intensities, judged by itself under the assumption (erroneous) that it is a valid index of reinforcement strength across intensities, might result in the conclusion of decreased strength or an increase in the aversive properties in the range of intensities beyond the peak of the self-stimulation rate-intensity function. The present results, produced by a completely different procedure, are entirely in agreement with those of Hodos and Valenstein (1962).

Aside from the generality of the data is the generality of the chaining technique itself. We have applied the chaining procedure in several studies on rewarding electrical stimulation of the brain. The procedure is useful over a broad range of variables, and the first member, VI rate has been found to be quite sensitive to changes in the values of experimental variables. As an analytical tool in the study of rewarding electrical stimulation of the brain, the chaining procedure nicely isolates the index of reinforcement strength from the side effects of stimulation. In addition, it makes contact with the techniques frequently employed in the experimental analysis of behavior employing more conventional reinforcers. In contrast, continuous reinforcement, which has been the dominant tool in brain-stimulation research, is rarely employed in the analysis of conventionally maintained behavior. Finally, the chaining procedure has been useful in generating intermittent schedule performances (maintained by brain-stimulation reward) at the values of schedule parameters often used with food reward.

The relationship between the ascending and descending curves might reflect either of, or a combination of, two factors. Since the VI rate determined at a particular extreme of the stimulation-intensity variable is lower when that determination was made later, rather than earlier, in a day's series of determinations, it is possible that the differences between the ascending and descending series were produced by a fatigue-like factor. On the other hand, the same differences between the ascending and descending series could represent an adaptation-to-stimulation effect. The higher intensities may have been less reinforcing because they were preceded by the lower intensities of stimulation (ascending series), and the lower intensities may have been less reinforcing because they were preceded by the higher intensities of stimulation (descending series). The first interpretation stresses the effects of previous responding, while the second stresses the effects of previous stimulation-reward. In either case, it would have to be assumed that the 20-min home-case rest (see above) between successive rate determinations was not sufficient to eliminate the effects of earlier determinations in the series.
