Nature of the 'stable' fraction of brain ATP

To obtain more information about the nature of the 'stable' fraction of brain ATP its stability was

compared with that ofadded ATP. ATP was added at 00 to (a) untreated samples of sucrose homogenates of brain tissue and to samples that had been either (b) heated to 100° for 10 min. and then chilled to 0° or (c) made 10 % (w/v) with respect to trichloroacetic acid. A control series to which no ATP was added were also prepared. After 3 min. trichloroacetic acid in a final concentration of 10 % (w/v) was added to samples (a) and (b) and all samples were centrifuged to give supernatants in which ATP was estimated in the usual way. The results are given in Table 7. It will be seen that added ATP was rapidly destroyed at 00 in the presence of untreated homogenate (line a), but was almost fully recovered if trichloroacetic acid had been added first (line c). The fact that the recovery was also increased by heating the sample at 1000 before addition ofATP (line b) suggests that the free ATP is destroyed by enzyme action. It follows that 'stable' ATP, including the fraction found in S3, must be insome way protectedagainst adenosine triphosphatase also present in the homogenate.

The regional distribution of 'stable' ATP shows no correlation with that of hydroxytryptamine or noradrenaline. Whereas 'stable' ATP is distributed fairly uniformly in both grey and white matter, both hydroxytryptamine and noradrenaline show marked regional variations in distribution, being present in highest concentration in the hypothalamus and in lower concentrations in the medulla, eaudate nucleus, thalamus, cerebral cortex and cerebellum (Bertler & Rosengren, 1959; Paton, 1958).

First, ATP might be involved ii4 the synthesis or binding of transmitter substances other than the

pressor amines, among them acetylcholine. However,theregionaldistributionofacetylcholine, either alone or summed with that of hydroxytryptamine and noradrenaline, does not correlate with that of ATP. Secondly, ATP may itself be a transmitter substance, as suggested by Holton (1959). Thirdly, the high concentration of ATP may simply reflect the survival in this fraction of the organized cytoplasm of a region of the neurone which would be expected tohaveahighrate ofmetabolism. Further work will be required before the significance of the ATP of the nerve-ending fraction is fully understood.

Reserpine failed to deplete the brain of 'stable' ATP in doses that almost completely depleted it of hydroxytryptamine. This is inmarked contrast with the effect of reserpine on the adrenal medulla (Schumann, 1958) and duodenal mucosa (Prusoff, 1961).

In the nerve endings ATP was found to be present in much higher concentration (about 210 ,um-moles/ g. of tissue) than hydroxytryptamine (about 1 ,ummole/g. of tissue); thus the ratio of ATP to amine was about 200: 1 and greatly exceeded the 1: 4 ratio to pressor amines found in non-nervous storage granules. Thus if there is a fraction of ATP concerned with the storage of pressor amines in stoicheiometric amounts similar to those found in non-nervous storage granules, this fraction would be completely masked by another fraction of ATP that is evidently not concemed directly with such storage. This is somewhat analogous to findings with platelets, which are also believed to contain a reserpine-insensitive fraction of ATP which raises the ATP to amine ratio well above 0-25 (Born, Ingram & Stacey, 1958).

SUMMARY

- 1. The contents of adenosine triphosphate of homogenates of normal guinea-pig brain in 0-32Msucrose and of fractions derived therefrom have been determined by the firefly method and compared with those of animals killed by immersion in liquid nitrogen and of animals pretreated with drugs.

The discovery of the herbicidal properties of 2,6-dichlorobenzonitrile (Barnsley & Rosher, 1961; Koopman & Daams, 1960) led to the synthesis of a number of related compounds, containing a 2,6dihalogenated phenyl nucleus, which had the same highly specific toxicity symptoms (Milborrow, 1963) as 2,6-dichlorobenzonitrile when applied to plants. The similarity of the plant response obtained with 2,6-dichlorobenzonitrile, 2,6-dichlorobenzaldoxime and a-amino-2,6-dichlorobenzaldoxime suggested that the last two compounds were converted into 2,6-dichlorobenzonitrile.
