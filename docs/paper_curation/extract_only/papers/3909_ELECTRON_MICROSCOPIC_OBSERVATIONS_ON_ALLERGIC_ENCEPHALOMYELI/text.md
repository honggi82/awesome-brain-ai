Electron microscopy has altered our concepts of the structure and formation of the myelin sheath.

It i now evident that myelin ia highly ordered multilayered membranous structure (1-6) which in the peripheral nervous system develops from and i an integral part of the Schwann c and in the central nervous system of the oligodendroglia. Formation of a myelin sheath i dependent upon the contiguous presence of both an axon and a sheath c Similarly, maintenance of the myelin sheath requires the presence of both neuronal and supporting elements, and consequently, injury to either should result in loss of the myelin. Thus, an insult to either the Schwann or oligodendroglial c should lead to a primary demyelination, whereas injury to the neuron or axon results first in degeneration of the axon and only secondarily in myelin destruction. Wallerian degeneration following section of a nerve or of a central tract ia prototype for the latter, secondary demyelination, and has been described electron microscopically for both the central and peripheral nervous systems (8 Concerning the former, primary demyelination in experimental allergic encephalomyelitis, it has been suggested on the basis of silver stained preparations that the axon i preserved in early l but that it has been denuded of its myelin sheath.

In view of the variety of spontaneously occurring human and animal demyelinating diseases and of the uncertainties concerning their origins, it is of interest to examine the fine structure in experimentally reproducible dernyelinization. With this in mind, we have examined allergic encephalomyelitis in rabbits by dectron microscopy.

DISCUSSION

Experimental allergic encephalomyelitis is a peculiarly instructive model for the study of demyelinization since its lesions bear a close resemblance to those of some human demyelinating diseases.

Addition of Freund's adjuvants ( to injected homologous or heterologous brain has made the production of demyelinating lesions ( more reproducible in comparison to the early experiments of Rivers, Sprunt, and Berry ( and of Rivers and Schwentker ( which were done without adjuvants. Waksman ( and Kabat, Wolf, and Bezer ( have shown that experimental allergic encephalomyelitis does not develop i the injected material i obtained from unmyelinated brains of immature animals. More recently materials partially purified chemically ( 18-20) or isolated cytologic fractions ( 2 have been injected in further attempts to characterize the antigenic material.

Even though the exact fraction of the injected material responsible for allergic encephalomyelitis i yet unknown, a characteristic response has been reproduced so frequently that it may be considered a pathological entity. The fine structural changes in the lesions of allergic encephalomyelitis are in distinct contrast to those of Wallerian

.noitareneged In the latter, dissolution of the axon i followed by fragmentation and phagocytosis of the myelin sheath and by proliferation of either Schwann c peripherally or fibrous astrocytes centrally. In both regions lipid i removed so slowly that myelin detritus i present for long periods after complete degeneration of the axon. In contrast, allergic encephalomyelitis i characterized by destruction of the myelin sheath with residual intact axons denuded of their myelin. Although attempts to obtain the earliest recognizeable l have not been carried out, the frequent and extensive swelling of oligodendroglial mitochondria in the absence of such changes in other c populations points toward an initial lesion in the oligodendroglia. S the central myelin sheath i an integral part of the oligodendroglial c it i not surprising that myelin degeneration should accompany such a g alteration. The observation that some axons were denuded on one side and not on the opposite i of considerable importance in relation to the manner in which central myelin i formed (Fig. 1 since it suggests that more than one oligodendroglial c may be involved in formation of a single nodal unit of central myelin.

We do not know why allergic encephalomyelitis involves the oligodendroglia and the myelin primarily. Certain p can, however be considered. The oligodendroglia subserve a double function in the central nervous system, forming the pathway for transport ( 2 and forming and maintaining the myelin sheath ( 6 O dendroglial c and their processes make many perivascular contacts (Fig. )31 so that they would be among the first c of the central nervous system exposed to circulating antibody. If oligodendroglia were altered by the antibody, as reflected in mitochondrial damage, their abnormality might be manifested by degeneration of myelin. Furthermore, since satellite oligodendroglia are related to nutrition of the neuron, some neurons or their axons might l undergo degeneration if their entire satellite population were d This might partially explain the spotty axonal degeneration which occasionally occurs concomitantly with myelin destruction.
