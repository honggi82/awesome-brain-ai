INTRODUCTORY

Researches in cortical Iocalisation by faradic stimulation culminated in the brilliant experiments of Sherrington and Griinbaum, reported in 1901 and 1903, and the changes which their work necessitated in the generally accepted ideas as to the extent of the motor areas in the ape and monkey, brought into prominence the histological method of Lewis and Clark. This had long been neglected because its use had given results not wholly in agreement with those obtained by electrical excitation. Knowledge of brain topography has already been greatly extended by this the newest branch of cerebral anatomy, and used in conjunction with other methods, it promises to throw light upon important problems in cerebral physiology.

Meynert, Exner and Wundt believed that all the cells of the cortex were morphologically and physiologically dike, and that differences in function were entirely dependent on the connections and associations of the cells (Brodmann).

In the reaction from this doctrine the tendency has*probably

been to lay too much stress on the specific functionsolindividual types and layers of cells. Brodmann enters an emphatic protest against this, insisting that only the most elementary kind of movement or sensory impression can take place from the stimulation of a single cell; more complicated functions cannot even be

restricted to a single area but must be regarded as involving a group of areas, or a region.

The general similarity of the cortical fields mapped out by Campbell, Brodmann and many others is remarkable in the series of animals investigated by them, but still there are striking variations. One of the areas most constantlypresent is the precentral, characterized by the layer of giant pyramidal cells frequently called, from the worker who first described them, Betz cells ('74). Betz regarded these as having some motor significance because of their form and distribution, and Lewis and Clark ('78), who confirmed and extended his work, took the same view. They found the giant pyramidal cells occupying certain areas corresponding closely to several of Ferrier's motor centers and by the histological method they mapped out the motor area for man with such accuracy that Campbell ('05), using a more complicated technique, has obtained approximately the same results. Facts subsequently brought to light have served to strengthen the original view that these cells were motor in lunction. The results of Campbell's examination of the brains from cases of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and amputations, especially, have added further weight to the theory. However, not until the researches of Gordon Holmes and Page May ('09) was it actually proved by the results of pathological and experimental lesions that the giant pyramidal cells do give origin to the greater part of the pyramid tract fibers, via.-those connected with the movements of the limbs and trunk.

Many other important questions regarding these prominent elements of the cerebral cortex are still awaiting solution, e.g., that relating to the meaning of the variations in their size in different animals; for why should the small coati have Betz cells as large as those of the bear and even of man, and why should they vary in different parts of the same motor cortex? Their numerous processes suggest complicated cortical connectionswhose significance is still obscure.

Lewis's pioneer work on comparative localisation by the histological method has received a fresh impetus during the last few years and not only the motor area but the entire cerebral cortex

METHOD OF PROCEDURE

In this investigation three of the five hemispheres examined were fixed in 95 per cent alcohol and the others in a 4 per cent formalin solution. The latter gave more satisfactary results since it prevented the shrinkage always resulting from alcohol fixation. The brains were photographed and the hemispheres divided by a coronal section from 5 to 6 mm. posterior to the cruciate sulcus. The entire hemisphere in front of this was dehydrated, cleared and imbedded in p a r a h as one piece or it was divided parallel with the original section into two or three pieces in order to facilitate penetration. This method obviated some of the difficulties of orientation which arises when the brain is divided into small pieces and in addition gave for comparison in

[Figure 4]

a single section, widely different cortical areas. No matter what the fixing fluid employed may be, a certain amount of shrinkage is inevitable due to the dehydration and the heat required for infiltration. However, in small pieces fixed in Ohlmacher’s solution and imbedded in celloidin-paraffin (H. D. King, ’10) the pyramidal cells were excellently preserved and no shrinkage was evident in them. The cell measurements were made from tissue prepgred in this manner.

The blocks of tissue were cut into serial sections from eight to fifteen microns thick and every tenth or twentieth mounted; most of the slides were stained by a modification of Nissl’s method in a 1per cent solution of toluidin blue. The differentiation was seldom carried as far as is desirable for the study of cell structure because the deeper color facilitates the location of specific layers. Hematoxylin and eosin were employed for occasional slides in two series which were of value for comparison.

Macroscopic measurements of the depth of the cortex were made on fresh brains with dividers, in order to get its appromimate thickness in different regions. Microscopic measurements are inaccurate because of unavoidable shrinkage of the tissue, yet the relative depth of the cell layers was obtained by using the filar micrometer and a three-fourth inch objective. In the measurements of cells the same micrometer and a one-eighth inch objective were employed. A detachable mechanical stage made greater accuracy possible.

The fourth layer, making up about one-fifth of the entire cortical depth, is characterized by a type of giant cell so striking from its size and contour that it readily makes this the most conspicuous lamina of the gyrus. The shape of these elements is much more irregular than that of the motor cells described for man, the monkey and carnivora, but the spindle-formed variety are most abundant. Their length is often more than twice their breadth and even with a nuclear stain the apical process can be followed almost to the molecular layer; the cell body is prolonged into it in such a manner that the limits of the two can scarcely be distinguished. The nucleus is relatively small and conforms to the shape of the cell. Another distinguishing feature is their richness in Nissl granules; because of the abundance of these the cells stain more deeply and can be readily located even when, as occurs in some parts, the size is not so distinctive. Including the apical and basal processes, Lewis distinguishes on an average slightly less than eight. Even hematoxylin and eosin or toluidin blue usually bring out four or five processes and in Golgi preparations eight and ten are frequently displayed. DISTRIBUTION

In the sheep and goat, and possibly in all ungulata, the giant pyramidal cells are found in the superior frontal gyrus which runs parallel with the longitudinal fissure. A smaller type, in four of the hemispheres examined, occupied both faces of the coronal sulcus for about the first (anterior) half centimeter of its extent, although more sparsely scattered on the lateral surface (fig. 3) where in the fifth series they were entirely absent. Posterior to this, two centimeters anterior to the cruciate fissure, they reach the surface of the convolution and a few extend over the mid-line to the parolfactory sulcus. A marked increase in their size and number occurs in sections directly posterior (fig. 4)and the cells are characteristically grouped into ‘nest$.’ Betz described this arrangement for the human cortex and since then it has been noticed in a number of animals. Three, four and often twice that number consititute a single group. At the bend of the fissure the clusters give way to a single file arrangement or the motor cells are entirely wanting. The maximum in size and number is reached as the splenial fissure is approached; on its anteriar face the crowding is such that the individual groups tend to blend with each other (fig. 5). Beyond this fissure they rapidly decrease in number (fig. 6) until the cruciate sulcus is reached; posterior to it, with one exception,giants cells are found only here and there. In this one series they continue, with a slight decrease in number, on both faces of the splenial fissure as far as the median termination of the cruciate sulcus and on the gyrus mediales for about 2 mm. They are absent on both surfacesof the coronal sulcusin this region.

The layer of polymorphous cells is nearly as deep as the preceding layer. It does not present any features of especial interest.

Compared with the report of Koppen and Lowenstein, I have found the area occupied by the giant cells more extensive anteriorly. Their first section of the motor type lies about a centimeter from the anterior end of the brain. On the medial face of the hemisphere the cells are always sparsely scattered and usually smaller than the average. Only occasionally have I found them beyond the parolfactory sulcus and never extending to the genual fissure (fissura cinguli of Burkholder) as these authors represent them.

CONCLUSIONS

The area of giant pyramidal cells corresponds closely with that part of the cortex which, on electrical stimulation, gives motor responses. In not a single instance, however, did we obtain a response when the electrodes were applied to the intermediate gyrus, although these elements were present on the lateral aspect of the coronal fissure throughout the greater part of its extent. Neither was the cortex behind the cruciate sulcus excitable and the single case, in which numerous Betz cells were found there, was doubtless an individual peculiarity. It is interesting that the largest cells are found in the region of the splenial fissure, the area from which limb movements were most easily obtained upon stimulation, while those in the head and face area (the anterior end of the gyrus) were much smaller.

The pyramid tract of the sheep, as I have found, is less highly developed than in the majority of animals investigated and in consideration of this the size of the Betz cells is somewhat surprising.

SUMMARY

The superior frontal convolution is characterized in the sheep’s brain by the elements analogous, from their size, shape and arrangement, with the motor cells described for man and a number of lower animals.
