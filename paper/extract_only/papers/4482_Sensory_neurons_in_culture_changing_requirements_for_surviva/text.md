ABSTRACT The effect of nerve growth factor (NGF) and medium conditioned by glioma cells (GCM) on the survival of chicken sensory neurons in culture was investigated. Neurons were isolated from embryos 8 days(E8)to 16days(E16)old and the proportion ofsurviving neurons was determined after2 days in culture. In the absence ofNGForGCM, essentially no neurons survived at any age. In the presence of NGF, survival increased from 25% of the neurons atES to 40% between E10and E12 and then decreased to background levels (5%) at E16. In contrast, in the presence of GCM, survival increased continuously from 10% of the neurons at ES to 75% at E16. At early developmental stages, the effect of NGF and GCM together was greater than the sum of their individual effects: at ES, about 80% of the neurons survived, double the number expected for a simple additive effect. Thus, a significant proportion of chicken neurons from dorsal root ganglia require both NGF and GCM for survival, and this may well include neurons from the ventro-lateral population, which do not respond to NGF alone. As neurons matured, the double requirement progressively decreased and, by E16, NGF no longer increased the number of neurons over that surviving in response to GCM alone. The facts that rat brain extracts mimicked the effect ofGCM and that the potency of the brain extracts of rat in the postnatal period increased in parallel with the development of the glial cells suggest thatglial cells produce a factor(s)both immunologically and functionally different from NGF which supports the survival of sensory neurons.

Medium conditioned by glioma cells contains a proteasesensitive factor able to support the survival of some sensory neurons isolated from dorsal root ganglia of chicken embryos (12). Thiseffectwasnotblockedby NGFantibodies, even when used in large excess. It was therefore of interest to determine if this glioma factor acts on the same neurons as those responding to NGF. This question was approached by comparing the effect of NGF and glioma-conditioned medium (GCM) on the survival of neurons from ganglia of different embryonic ages. The results provide evidence that NGF and the factor contained in GCM can bedistinguished on the basisof the age of the neurons that respond tothem. In addition, it isshown that combination of both factors between E8 and E16 leads to the survival in vitro ofessentially all theneuronsdissociated from chicken dorsal root ganglia. Furthermore, evidence is presented that an activity similar to that contained in GCM is present in extracts of rat brain, the amount of activity being dependent upon the age of the brain. Because the development of glia is largely postnatal in the rat, this observed increase in activity is consistent with its glial origin.

During embryogenesis, neurons of the peripheral nervous system depend for theirsurvival and differentiation on specific factors (for a recent review, see ref. 1). The most striking evidence for such a dependence is that antibodies to nerve growth factor (NGF), the only purified and well-characterized factor, lead to a degeneration of sympathetic neurons when injected into newborn mammals (2). Conversely, injection of NGF prevents the death of neurons, which normally occurs during the development of sympathetic ganglia (3). In addition, the absolute requirement of target neurons for NGF can be demonstrated in cultures of isolated neurons: sympathetic neurons isolated from newborn mammals or chicken embryos do not survive in nitro unless NGF isadded to the culture medium (4, 5). The same dependence on the presence of NGF has been shown for sensory neurons isolated from dorsal root ganglia of newborn mammals (6) and chicken embryos (5, 7) during a restricted period of development.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Neuron Cultures. The cultures were established by the method described by Greene (7), with the modifications described here. Eggs of Arbor Acres hens were incubated at 37.5°C in a 70% water-saturated incubator and rotated four times per day. Hatching under these conditions occurred between day 20 and day 21 of incubation. Embryos 8, 10, 12, 14, and 16days old were used afterstaging according tothecriteria of Hamburger and Hamilton (13). Seventy-six to 80 ganglia were dissected from two embryos. In the thoracic region, care was taken to separate the dorsal root from the sympathetic ganglia that lie in close apposition. The dorsal root ganglia were collected in 1 ml of F-14 medium (made from GIBCO F-12 powder supplemented as in ref. 14), which at the end of the dissection was replaced by 2 ml of Ca2+- and Mg2+-free phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.1% trypsin (Worthington). After 20 min of incubation at 37°C, the ganglia were centrifuged and washed twice with F-14 medium containing 10% (vol/vol) heat-inactivated horse serum (GIBCO). RESULTS

Effects of NGF and GCM on Survival of Sensory Neurons of Different Embryonic Ages. Fig. 2 shows that between E8 and E16, virtually no neurons survived in the absence of NGF and GCM. Addition of NGF (5 ng/ml) led to a maximum of about 40% of neurons surviving from E1O-E12 embryos. Higher NGF concentrations did not increase the survival. With older embryos, this percentage decreased markedly toapproach control values by E16. In contrast, the response to GCM was small at the beginning (about 12% at E8) and subsequently increased continuously with age until E16, when more than 70% of the cells platedsurvived. Tested at E12, higher concentrations of GCM did not have a bigger effect. In all cases, neurons were counted after 48 hr in culture. At this stage, they had formed long processes (see Fig. 1) and could be kept for at least another 2 days without any significant drop in their numbers. By that time, the processes were longer and the rare nonneuronal cells showed obvious signs of degeneration, in particular, numerous vacuoles in their cytoplasm.

Cultures were maintained at 370C in a water-saturated atmosphere with 8% CO2. When cells were cultured for more than 2 days, the medium was changed after 2 days.

Effects of Combination of NGF plus GCM. In view of the possibility that NGF and GCM might act on different neurons, the effect of the combination of both NGF and GCM was examined. At early ages such a combination resulted in the survival of more cells than those supported by either NGF or GCM alone (Table 1). In fact, at E8, the effect was substantially more than additive [about 80%, rather than 38% (from 12% and 26% for GCM and NGF, respectively)], suggesting that at this stage, some neurons require the combination of both factors tosurvive in culture. As the neurons matured in vivo, this requirement progressively disappeared and, by E16, most of the neurons survived with GCM alone (Fig. 2 and Table 1).

Effects of Brain Extracts and Brain Extracts plus NGF. Because GCM is the product of glial tumor cells in culture, it was of interest to test whether extracts of a tissue rich in glia, such as the brain, exhibit a similar activity. Table 1 shows that rat brain extracts indeed supported the survival of sensory neurons in culture. Furthermore, as with GCM, this effect increased markedly with the maturation of the sensory ganglia. Also, at early embryonic ages, as with GCM, the combination of brain extracts with NGF was more than additive. However, it is not known if higher concentrations ofbrain extracts would result in an increased survival: at concentrations above 25 mg/ml, the adult brain extracts were toxic and resulted in a lower survival.

Because the number of glial cells in rat brain markedly increases during the weeks after birth (19), it was of interest to see ifa correlation existed between the postnatal ageofthebrain and the number of surviving neurons. Fig. 3 shows that such a correlation does indeed exist. There was an approximately 4-fold increase in the activity of brains between postnatal days 2 and 30. Unlike the extracts of adult brains, those of early postnatal rats were not toxic when used at concentrations higher than 25 mg/ml. Indeed, by increasing the concentration of young rat brain extracts to 50and 100 mg/ml, it proved possible to obtain the same level of survival as was seen with 25 mg of adult brain extracts per ml.

DISCUSSION

The results presented here show that neurons dissociated from the dorsal root ganglia of chicken embryos of different ages have different requirements for factors necessary for their survival in culture. Thus, a maximum of about 40% neurons survive when cultured in the presence of NGF between E10 and E12. Subsequently, this percentage decreases very markedly. These results agree with the observations that explants of dorsal root ganglia from chicken embryos older than E1S-E16 no longer respond to NGF by extending processes (20, 21), which is correlated with a marked decrease of NGF receptors during this period (21). Thus, the increasing effect of GCM or brain extracts on older sensory neurons allows a clear distinction to be made between NGF and the factor(s) contained in GCM orbrain extracts not only immunologically, but also functionally in terms of differential neuronal sensitivity.

At all embryonic ages between E8 and E16, the combination of NGFplus GCM orbrain extractsleads to thesurvivalofabout 75-90% of thecells plated. Taking into account a small, variable number of nonneuronal cells not removed by preplating and included in the number of cells plated, virtually all the neurons (excluding trypan blue) plated can survive at all ages between E8 and E16. According to the scanty information available on the number of neurons in dorsal root ganglia of the chicken [8000-12,000 neurons in the 25th lumbar ganglia in E8embryos (8, 25)], the yields reported here indicate that there is little loss of neurons during the dissociation of the ganglia. It is therefore worth speculating on which population of neurons responds to which factor.

From experiments performed both in vivo (10) and on explants of dorsal root ganglia in vitro (11), it is known that only the medio-dorsal neurons respond to NGF. If the isolated neurons supported by NGF are even only a part of the mediodorsal population, given the very high overall survival in the presence of NGF and GCM or brain extracts, those neurons that at early ages require the combination of NGF plus the factor(s) contained in GCM or brain extracts include at least part of the ventro-lateral population. This would mean that early in the development of the chicken dorsal root ganglia, the ventrolateral neurons are also a target for NGF, but in contrast to at least some of the medio-dorsal neurons, NGF alone is not enough to permit their survival in culture.

As the ganglia mature in vivo, the morphological distinction between the two populations becomes less clear and by E15 is no longer possible (8). The present study shows that as the sensory neurons mature, they become less dependent on NGF (either alone or in combination) and that at E16 they can be supported by GCM or brain extracts alone.

At present, we have only indirect evidence that the factor(s) contained in brain extracts comes from glia: a similar activity is found in medium conditioned by glioma cells and, further, mature rat brain, which contains more glia than newborn, also contains more activity. In the dorsal root ganglia, a source could be the satellite cells that completely ensheath the neurons as early as E10 in the chicken embryo (26).
