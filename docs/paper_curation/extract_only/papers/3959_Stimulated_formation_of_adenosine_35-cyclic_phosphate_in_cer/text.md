# Stimulated Formation of Adenosine 3',5'-Cyclic Phosphate in Cerebral Cortex: Synergism between Electrical Activity and Biogenic Amines

#### Hirotoshi Shimizu, Cyrus R. Creveling, and John Daly

NATIONAL INSTITUTES OF HEALTH

Communicated by Bernhard Witkop, January 8, 1970

Abstract. When cortical slices are incubated with adenine-"4C, adenine nucleotides are labeled in a small and relatively stable pool. The ATP-14C of this pool is readily converted to cAMP-'4C during incubations with depolarizing agents, such as K+, ouabain, veratridine, or batrachotoxin. During incubations with these agents, release of acetylcholine and of adenosine into the medium is enhanced. The increase in release of adenosine parallels the enhanced formation of cAMP-'4C elicited by depolarizing agents, providing further evidence that adenosine may serve to couple electrical activity in the central nervous system with formation of cAMP. When adenosine or a depolarizing agent are incubated, together with a biogenic amine, such as histamine, serotonin, or norepinephrine, the combined effect on cAMP-14C formation in cortical slices is much more than additive. Extracellular levels of biogenic amines could in this manner modulate cAMP formation and biochemical responses in nervous tissue during electrical activity.

The key role of adenosine 3',5'-cyclic phosphate (cATMP); in regulation of biochemical responses of cells, tissues, or organs to external stimuli has been firmly established in a great variety of biological systems.1 The central nervous system contains high levels of adenyl cyclase2 and phosphodiesterase,3 the enzymes responsible, respectively, for the formation and degradation of cAMP. In addition, a variety of external stimuli; i.c., electrical stimulation,4 histamine, and norepinephrine,5' 6 increase levels of endogenous cAMP in brain slices. However, the role of cAMP in brain remains an enigma. The present paper is concerned with the possible significance of a synergistic effect of electrical activity and elevated extracellular concentrations of biogenic amines on the formation of cAMP in cortical slices.

Discussion. Incubation of brain slices with biogenic amines such as histamine or norepinephrine4' I or with high concentration of K+13 results in elevated levels of cAMP. Electrical stimulation of slices has the same effect.4 These effects appear to be the result of stimulation of adenyl cyclase rather than inhibition of metabolism of cAMP. Histamine and norepinephrine stimulate adenyl cyclase in cerebellar slices through independent regulatory units5' 6 but do not appear to be involved in the stimulation of adenyl cyclase, which results from electrical stimulation4 or incubation of slices with depolarizing agents.'3 15 The latter effects appear due to another regulatory unit, sensitive to adenosine and desensitized by theophylline.4' 13, 16 This adenosine-sensitive regulatory system is capable of interacting synergistically with both norepinephrine- and histamine-sensitive systems, i.e., the enhanced formation of cAMP, seen during electrical stimulation in the presence of either histamine or norepinephrine4 or during incubations with adenosine and either histamine or norepinephrine,16 is much more than additive.

The present and preceding papers7' 14, 15 have demonstrated that brain slices, after incubation with adenine-14C, contain pool(s) of adenine-'4C nucleotides, which are excellent precursors of cAMIP-14C. Incubation of such prelabeled slices of guinea pig cerebral cortex with depolarizing agents15 converts 20 per cent of labeled ATP to cAIP-14C. Incubation of the same slices with a biogenic amine, such as histamine, converts 4 per cent of the labeled ATP "pool" to cAVMP-14C (Fig. 1). Incubation with combinations of depolarizing agents and amines, however, results in a conversion of up to 40 per cent of the labeled nucleotides to cAMP-14C (Figs. 1 and 2). Under similar conditions, the elevated intracellular levels of cAM\P (-'0.04 mM)4 represent <5 per cent of the total endogenous levels of ATP (1-2 mM).17 This suggests that the pool of ATP, labeled by prior incubation with adenine-14C, is highly compartmentalized and represents <10 per cent of the total ATP of the brain slices. This pool does not readily equilibrate with other pools of ATP, since the percentage conversion of ATP-14C to cAMP-14C, evoked during incubations with veratridine and histamine, does not decrease even after prior incubation of labeled slices for 50 minutes before the introduction of veratridine and histamine (Fig. 3). Under conditions of extensive depletion of ATP in such a compartmentalized pool, reversal of the adenyl cyclase reaction (ATP v± cAMP + pyrophosphate) becomes feasible. The equilibrium of this reaction has been demonstrated to favor ATP formation,18' 19 but its reversal has yet to be demonstrated in a mammalian system.

At present, the most plausible mechanism for depolarization-induced stimulation of cAMP is the one proposed by Rall'6; i.e., depolarization results in release of adenosine, which then stimulates cAMP formation. Evidence consists of the following: (i) Stimulation of cAiMIP formation by either adenosine or by depolarization is blocked by theophylline. 15.16 (ii) Stimulation by depolarization requires Ca++lS and can be blocked by Mg++,l" suggesting an obligatory calcium mediated coupling of depolarization to a "transmitter" release. Stimulation by adenosine does not require Ca++20 and cannot be blocked by MIg++.ll 20 (iii) Synergistic effects on the stimulation of cAlVIP formation occur when either depolarizing agents or adenosine are incubated together with either histamine or norepinephrine (refs. 4 and 16, and Figs. 1 and 2). (iv) During the incubation of slices previously labeled with adenine-'4C, adenosine-14C is continuously released into the medium (Fig. 6). Spontaneous release of adenosine-14C is increased slightly during incubations with K+ and markedly during incubations with veratridine or ouabain, roughly in parallel with enhanced formation of cAMP-'4C elicited by the same agents (Figs. 6 and 2).

An alternate or additional interpretation of depolarization-induced formation of cAMP is that it is involved in calcium-coupled release2l of acetylcholine in nerve synapses. Indeed, conditions of depolarization cause both calciumdependent release of acetylcholine (Fig. 4) and calcium-dependent formation of cAMP.'5 A presynaptic role for cAM/IP in such a release process has been suggested22' 23 and has precedent in the cAMNIP-mediated release of insulin in the pancreas,24 amylase in the salivary gland,25 and ACTH in the pituitary gland.26 The increase in acetylcholine release evoked by depolarizing agents does not, however, parallel enhanced formation of cAMP-'4C (Figs. 4 and 2). The greatly enhanced formation of cAMP during membrane depolarization in the presence of biogenic amines would appear to be of fundamental significance to an understanding of the role of cAMP in the central nervous system. Depolarizing agents stimulate cAMP formation in cortical slices from a variety of species, usually to a greater extent than biogenic amines. This might imply that the primary control of cAMP formation is linked to the passage of nerve impulses across the synapses, but that elevation of cAMP levels by such electrical activity can be increased by the concomitant presence of histamine, norepinephrine, serotonin or perhaps of other neurohormones. Such interrelations and the localization of adenyl cyclase and phosphodiesterase at synaptic regions28 would provide a mechanism whereby biogenic amines could regulate the biochemical response of an individual (cholinergic) synapse to electrical activity.

Unfortunately, little is as yet known of the subsequent biological function(s) for cAMP in the central nervous system. In analogy to the role for cAMP in other organs, i.e., the regulation of a fundamental and specific function, cAMIP may well play a role in the regulation of electrical activity in brain. Indeed, N,O-dibutyryl cAMIP causes an increase in discharge rates in the neuromuscular junction,23 electroencephalographic changes after injection into cat cerebral ventricles,29 and a reduction in the spontaneous discharge rate in the Purkinje cell of rat cerebellumA3 In brain preparations cAMP has beendemonstrated to stimulate glutamate-N-acetylase,3' glutamate dehydrogenase32 and a protein kinase capable of phosphorylating histones.33 Whether such biochemical pathways regulated by cAMP could indeed serve to modulate electrical activity or modify subsequent synaptic efficiency in the central nervous system, or whether as yet unknown mechanisms are involved in the function of cAMP in this complex system remains an important challenge for research in neurochemistry.
