1. Hexokinase activities were estimated in primary subcellular fractions from guinea-pig cerebral cortex and in sucrose-density-gradient subfractions of the mitochondrial and microsomal fractions. 2. Appreciable activities were observed in mitochondrial, microsomal and soluble fractions. The activity in the mitochondrial fraction was associated with the mitochondria rather than with myelin ornerve endings andthatinthemicrosomal fractionwas associatedwithmembrane fragments. 3. Most ofthe mitochondrial activity was extracted in soluble form by osmotic 'shock'. The activity ofthe mitochondrial extract differed from the soluble activity in kinetic properties and in electrophoretic behaviour. 4. No evidence was obtained for the presence ofa high-K. glucokinase in the brain. 5. The results are discussed in terms ofrelevance to considerations ofglucose utilization by the brain.

Since the studies by Crane & Sols (1953) on brain hexokinase (ATP-D-hexose 6-phosphotransferase, EC 2.7.1.1), it has been generally accepted that this enzyme activity is associated mainly with the mitochondria, in contrast with other tissues where hexokinase is found mainly in the cytoplasmic or soluble fraction. In view ofthe special requirement for glucose by the brain and its rapid utilization therein, the distribution ofglucose-phosphorylating systems is of particular interest, particularly as it has been suggested (Lowry & Passonneau, 1964) that hexokinase is one of the control points of glycolysis in the brain.

The present paper reports the results of tissuefractionation and sucrose-density-gradient studies of glucose phosphorylation in brain. Kinetic experiments and electrophoretic separations of multiple forms ofthe enzyme have been carried out on different subfractions. A preliminary conmmunication has been given (Bachelard, 1967).

The concentration of intracellular glucose in the brain is particularly difficult to assess in view ofthe conflicting reports of the overall glucose content of brain. Gey (1956) separated glucose from other reducing substances by paper chromatography of extracts prepared from unanaesthetized rat brain frozenin8itu, andreportedtheglucose contentto be 8mg./100g. of tissue. Lowry, Passonneau, Hasselberger & Schultz (1964) found the brain glucose content to be 20-27mg./lOOg. in anaesthetized mice, also after freezing in situ. The blood volume ofwhole brain has been estimated to be about 3% of the tissue volume (Everett, Simmons & Lasher, 1956; Rosomoff, 1961) and the cerebrospinal-fluid volumetobe about 9% (Rosomoff, 1961). Assuming a mean blood glucose content of 90mg./lOOml. and a cerebrospinal-fluid glucose content of 70mg./ lOOml. (Davson, 1955; Cooper & Archdeacon, 1960), the contribution of these fluids to the apparent glucose content of whole brain would be about 9mg./lOOg. Any glucose present in the extracellular non-vascular space of the brain (4-25% of the tissue volume; Barlow, Domek, Goldberg & Roth, 1961; Gibson & McIlwain, 1965) has also to be takenintoaccount,soitseemspossible that the net intracellular glucose concentration of the brain may be very low, and if the estimated glucose content of Gey (1956) is accepted, may even approach zero. Though such a calculation is based on considerations of the brain as a whole, without regard to differences in cell types, it does indicate the possibility that all of the glucose available for glycolysis in the brain is phosphorylated immediately on entry, which would be difficult to envisageifthephosphorylating siteswereessentially mitochondrial. However, the enzyme in the microsomalfraction (presumablyderivedfromtheplasma membrane and the endoplasmic reticulum) and particularly in the cell cytoplasm together total nearly half of the hexokinase activity present (i.e. about 550-600,umoles/g./hr.), and should be more than sufficient to account for all of the glucose phosphorylated (Lowry & Passoneau, 1964).

###### DISCUSSION

Brain hexokinase has generally been accepted as being mainly particulate in occurrence, and most studies have used aparticulate fraction prepared by centrifugation ofhomogenates at 18000gfor 30min. based on the work ofCrane & Sols (1953). However, the results of Stern (1954) indicated that not all of the glucose-phosphorylating activity is associated with particulate fractions. Kerly & Leaback (1957) found that only one-third of the hexokinase was sedimented by centrifuging as above, and Johnson (1960) found that 20% was non-particulate. In the present study, of the activity recovered in subcellular fractions from sucrose homogenates, 40% appeared in the primary mitochondrial fraction, over 25% inthenon-particulate soluble fraction and nearly 20% in the microsomal fraction. The results from density-gradient centrifugation of the microsomal fraction indicated that the activity was associated with membrane structures. The greater part of the microsomal activity would be likely to have appeared in the particulate fraction obtained by Crane & Sols (1953), prepared at 18000g for 20min., since a large proportion of the membrane fragments of microsomal preparations have been shown to be sedimented under similar conditions (Schwartz et al. 1962).

The results from density-gradient centrifugation of the primary mitochondrial fraction suggested thatthe activity is associatedwiththe mitochondria rather than with the other constituents that occur there, such as myelin or nerve endings. The distribution of acetylcholinesterase (enriched in nerve endings; De Robertis et al. 1962) differed markedly from the hexokinase distribution in the mitochondrial subfractions. The ready release, by osmotic 'shock', ofthe hexokinase activity from the mitochondria suggests that the enzyme is relatively weakly bound, and in the nomenclature suggested by Egger & Rapoport (1963) is 'non-sessile'.
