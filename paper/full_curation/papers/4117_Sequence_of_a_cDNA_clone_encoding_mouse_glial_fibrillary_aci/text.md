ABSTRACT A clone encoding mouse glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) was isolated from a cDNA library constructed so as to express the cloned sequences. The library was screened using a GFAP-specific polyclonal antiserum; a single bacterial colony expressing GFAP was identified. The complete sequence of the cDNA insert in this clone is presented, encompassing 2.5 kilobases and specifying >97% ofthe GFAP amino acid sequence. The clone includes a long (1.4-kilobase) 3' untranslated region. Within the coding region, the data show extensive homology with other intermediate filament proteins, particularly in those regions predicted to be a-helical. RNA blot transfer experiments using the cloned GFAP cDNA probe revealed a single GFAP mRNA species of 2.7 kilobases in mouse brain. Southern blot analysis indicates the existence of at most two genes encoding GFAP in the mouse genome. The mouse GFAP probe cross-hybridizes weakly at high stringency with genomic DNA from diverse eukaryotic species.

(7). The resulting cDNA expression library contained about 40,000 clones, with an average insert size of 1 kb (data not shown). Because the cDNA is inserted into the lacZ gene of the plasmid, it is transcribed from the plasmid promoter and expressed in the bacteria as afusion protein. If the cDNA is inserted in the correct reading frame and is of sufficient length, the resulting fusion protein is potentially detectable by antibodies to the protein of interest. We screened our library with a monospecific polyclonal rabbit anti-bovine GFAP antiserum (6) and found one positive clone.

Five distinct classes of intermediate filaments have been identified, each characteristic ofa different cell type in higher eukaryotes. Keratins are expressed in epithelial cells; neurofilaments, in neurons; glial filaments, in astroglial cells; desmin, in muscle cells; and vimentin, mostly in cells of mesenchymal origin (for review, see refs. 1 and 2). These proteins polymerize to form 10-nm filaments that, together with tubulin and actin, constitute the cytoskeleton. From peptide mapping and protein sequence data, a common structural model for intermediate filament subunits is emerging. This model is comprised of an a-helical central domain having significant amino acid homology among all the intermediate filaments, with nonhelical heads and tails unique to each class (3, 4).

Among the classes of intermediate filament protein subunits, least is known about glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP). This protein is of particular interest because it represents a specific marker in the development of the central nervous system, its presence distinguishing astrocytes from other glial cell types. Here we describe the isolation and complete sequence of a 2.5-kilobase (kb) cDNA clone encoding mouse GFAP. The structural and evolutionary implications of the predicted amino acid sequence of mouse GFAP are discussed.

The fusion protein immunoprecipitated from GFAP-expressing bacteria uniformly labeled with a 14C-labeled amino acid mixture is shown in Fig. 1 (lane 9). The data show a product identical in size to the selected, translated GFAP protein. This result suggests that G1 is missing sequences coding for approximately the first 12 amino acids of GFAP, since the fusion protein consists of 12 amino acids from the lacZ gene of the pUC9 plasmid at its amino terminus followed by the amino acids coded for by the inserted cDNA.

The GFAP cDNA has a Sal I linker at the 5' end, but the EcoRI linker expected to be at the 3' end is absent. The precise nature of the genetic event leading to this absence is uncertain. The EcoRI linker may have changed as a consequence ofa single base substitution or as the result ofa deletion encompassing one or several base pairs incurred during the cloning procedure. The sequence data show no evidence of a mutated EcoRI site adjacent to the vector. A deletion cannot have involved other than a small portion of the construct, because the cDNA insert is 2.5 kb long and known to be missing its 5' untranslated region along with the sequences encoding approximately 12 NH2-terminal amino acids (see above) while the mouse GFAP mRNA is only 2.7 kb long (Fig. 2A, lane 1). In addition, a probe encompassing the 3' terminal 650 base pairs ofthe cloned cDNA also detects a 2.7-kb band in an RNA blot-transfer experiment (Fig. 2A, lane 2) and therefore contains authentic mouse GFAP mRNA sequences.

DISCUSSION Structural data on a variety of intermediate filament proteins from several species point to the existence of a large multigene family encoding these polypeptides. In the case of GFAP, only limited sequence data encompassing a 28-amino acid peptide of the bovine protein have been reported (20). In this paper, we describe the nucleic acid sequence of a cloned cDNA, G1, encompassing >97% ofthe coding region and the bulk of the 3' untranslated region of mouse GFAP

#### mRNA.

The amino acid sequence ofthe GFAP predicted from the cDNA sequence of G1 reveals strong homology with other intermediate filament proteins, especially desmin and vimentin (Fig. 3). This homology is expected both on the basis ofpeptide mapping data (21) and because GFAP copolymerizes with vimentin and desmin in vivo. The homology is greatest (65% for desmin, 67% for vimentin) in the three ahelical regions postulated in the model for intermediate filaments by Geisler and co-workers (4, 21). The heptade repeat seen in the other proteins is present in GFAP, resulting in a helix with 3.5 residues per turn and a hydrophobic backbone. The hydrophobic backbone is postulated to cause aggregation of GFAP monomers into coiled coils, which then polymerize into filaments (4). Where the hydrophobic backbone is interrupted by a charged residue in the other intermediate filament proteins, the same is often true in GFAP, suggesting that these locations may be of functional significance. Where the amino acids differ in the a-helical region, the charge or nonpolarity at that position in the peptide is usually preserved. Like desmin, the charged and uncharged residues in the helix fall into clusters with approximately a 28-residue (40 A) repeat (21). As noted by Geisler and Weber (4), the extensive conservation among intermediate filament proteins in their a-helical domains probably reflects the necessity of maintaining both the 7-residue repeat pattern and the charge clustering during the course of evolution.

With the exception of some epidermal keratins (19) and vimentin (17), the bulk of intermediate filament protein sequence data have been derived by direct protein sequencing. The availability ofa GFAP clone will enable further study of the genetics, expression, and development regulation of this protein, as well as its role in glial cell differentiation.

The amino and carboxyl termini of intermediate filaments are less homologous than the a-helical central region. These regions may confer on the intermediate filaments their structural, functional, and antigenic individuality. Because of the extensive homology among all known intermediate filament proteins, it is somewhat surprising that many intermediate filament polyclonal antisera are monospecific. This observation suggests that either the nonhomologous end domains of the proteins contain the strongest antigenic determinants or minor differences in the homologous regions are responsible for antigenic specificity. The amino acid sequence of mouse GFAP, as predicted from the G1 sequence, is consistent with these arguments. About 12 amino acids are missing from the cDNA sequence at the NH2 terminus (see above), but that portion of the amino-terminal domain (Fig. 3) whose sequence is known shows no homology with other intermediate filament proteins.
